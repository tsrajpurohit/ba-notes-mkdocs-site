    Module 2 -- Agile Frameworks

    Lesson 14: Introduction to Agile Frameworks

    Learning objectives

    By the end of this lesson, you will understand:

    • What an Agile framework is.

    • Why Agile frameworks are needed.

    • The relationship between Agile values and frameworks.

    • The major Agile frameworks you will study.

    • How to choose an appropriate framework for a project.

    • How Agile frameworks apply to healthcare, pharmacovigilance, and AI product development.

    What is an Agile framework?

    An Agile framework is a structured approach that helps teams apply Agile values and principles in their day-to-day work.

    While Agile provides the mindset and philosophy, frameworks provide the practical structure for implementing that mindset.

    Simple definition: An Agile framework is a set of practices, roles, workflows, and guidelines that help teams deliver value iteratively and adapt to change.

    Relationship between Agile and frameworks

    Agile

    Values and principles

    ↓

    Agile frameworks

    Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean, SAFe, etc.

    ↓

    Team practices

    Sprints, boards, reviews, retrospectives, flow management

    Agile is the philosophy; frameworks are the methods used to apply that philosophy.

    Why Agile frameworks are needed

    Agile values and principles provide direction, but teams also need practical guidance on:

    • How to organize work.

    • How to plan and prioritize tasks.

    • How to collaborate with stakeholders.

    • How to track progress.

    • How to improve continuously.

    Example:

    • The Agile principle says: "Deliver working software frequently."

    • A framework such as Scrum explains how to do this through Sprints.

    • A framework such as Kanban explains how to do this through continuous flow.

    Major Agile frameworks

    Framework Primary focus

    Scrum Structured iterative development with defined roles and events.

    Kanban Visual workflow management and continuous flow.

    Lean Eliminating waste and maximizing customer value.

    Extreme Technical excellence and engineering practices. Programming

    Crystal People-centric approach tailored to team size and project criticality.

    DSDM Agile project delivery with business involvement and fixed time/cost.

    SAFe Scaling Agile practices across large organizations.

    LeSS Scaling Scrum across multiple teams.

    Nexus Coordinating multiple Scrum teams working on one product.

    Spotify model Autonomous squads and organizational alignment.

    Simple analogy

    Agile as the philosophy

    Agile is like the philosophy of healthy living:

    • Stay active.

    • Eat nutritious food.

    • Adapt habits based on health needs.

    • Continuously improve your lifestyle.

    Frameworks as specific methods

    • Scrum is like a structured workout program with scheduled sessions.

    • Kanban is like a daily activity tracker that helps you maintain a steady flow.

    • Lean is like removing unhealthy habits to improve efficiency.

    • XP is like focusing on high-quality training techniques.

    All support the same goal, but each provides a different way to achieve it.

    Overview of frameworks

    Scrum

    • Uses fixed-length Sprints.

    • Defines roles such as Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers.

    • Includes events such as Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective.

    • Best for teams that need structure and regular feedback cycles.

    Kanban

    • Visualizes work on a Kanban board.

    • Uses WIP limits to control work in progress.

    • Focuses on continuous flow rather than fixed iterations.

    • Best for support, maintenance, and continuous delivery work.

    Lean

    • Focuses on eliminating waste.

    • Improves workflow efficiency.

    • Maximizes customer value.

    • Best for organizations seeking process optimization.

    Extreme Programming (XP)

    • Emphasizes technical excellence.

    • Uses practices such as pair programming, test-driven development (TDD), and continuous integration.

    • Best for projects requiring high code quality and rapid feedback.

    Scaled frameworks

    • SAFe helps large organizations coordinate many Agile teams.

    • LeSS applies Scrum principles to multiple teams.

    • Nexus focuses on integrating work from several Scrum teams.

    • Spotify model emphasizes autonomous squads and alignment.

    How to choose an Agile framework

    Project situation Suitable framework

    Team needs structured iterations and roles. Scrum

    Work arrives continuously and priorities change Kanban frequently.

    Organization wants to reduce waste and improve Lean efficiency.

    Project requires strong engineering practices and Extreme Programming code quality. (XP)

    Multiple teams must coordinate on one product. SAFe, LeSS, or Nexus

    Healthcare example

    Hospital Management System

    • Scrum can be used to develop modules such as patient registration, appointment scheduling, and prescription management in Sprints.

    • Kanban can be used for ongoing support tickets and bug fixes.

    • Lean can help reduce delays in patient workflow processes.

    Pharmacovigilance example

    For your AI Pharmacovigilance Case Processing Platform:

    • Scrum would be useful for developing major features such as ICSR management, AI data extraction, MedDRA coding, and reporting.

    • Kanban would be useful for managing continuous regulatory updates, bug fixes, and AI model improvements.

    • Lean would help optimize the case processing workflow and reduce manual effort.

    AI product example

    For an AI Medical Chatbot:

    • Scrum can manage planned feature development.

    • Kanban can manage continuous model improvements and user feedback requests.

    • XP can ensure high-quality code and automated testing.

    Common misconceptions

    • "Agile and Scrum are the same." Scrum is one Agile framework, but Agile includes many frameworks.

    • "One framework is best for all projects." The best framework depends on team size, project complexity, workflow type, and organizational context.

    • "Frameworks replace Agile mindset." Frameworks are tools; they are effective only when supported by Agile values and principles.

    Best practices

    • Understand the Agile mindset before selecting a framework.

    • Choose a framework based on project needs, team structure, and workflow characteristics.

    • Start with Scrum if the team needs clear structure and iterative delivery.

    • Use Kanban for continuous flow and support work.

    • Combine frameworks when appropriate, such as Scrumban.

    • Continuously inspect and adapt the framework implementation.

    Interview questions

    Q1. What is an Agile framework?

    Answer: An Agile framework is a structured approach that provides practices, roles, workflows, and guidelines for applying Agile values and principles in product development and project management.

    Q2. What is the difference between Agile and an Agile framework?

    Answer: Agile is a mindset and set of values and principles, while an Agile framework is a practical method for implementing those values and principles.

    Q3. Name some common Agile frameworks.

    Answer: Common Agile frameworks include Scrum, Kanban, Lean, Extreme Programming (XP), Crystal, DSDM, SAFe, LeSS, Nexus, and the Spotify model.

    Summary

    Agile frameworks provide the practical structure needed to apply Agile values and principles in real projects. Different frameworks are suitable for different situations:

    • Scrum provides structured iterative development.

    • Kanban manages continuous workflow.

    • Lean improves efficiency by reducing waste.

    • XP enhances technical quality.

    • SAFe, LeSS, and Nexus help scale Agile across multiple teams.

    For healthcare, pharmacovigilance, and AI product development, the choice of framework should depend on the nature of the work, team structure, regulatory requirements, and need for continuous feedback.

    Key takeaways

    • Agile is the mindset; frameworks are the practical implementation methods.

    • Scrum is best for structured iterative development.

    • Kanban is best for continuous flow work.

    • Lean is best for process optimization and waste reduction.

    • XP is best for improving technical excellence.

    • Framework selection should be based on project context, team needs, and workflow characteristics.

    What is Scrum?

    Scrum is an Agile framework used to develop, deliver, and sustain complex products through iterative and incremental work.

    Instead of building the entire product at once, Scrum teams work in short cycles called Sprints, usually lasting 1 to 4 weeks.

    Simple definition: Scrum is a lightweight Agile framework that helps teams deliver valuable product increments through collaboration, transparency, inspection, and adaptation.

    Purpose of Scrum

    Scrum was designed to help teams:

    • Deliver value quickly through small, usable increments.

    • Adapt to changing requirements without disrupting the entire project.

    • Improve collaboration between business and technical teams.

    • Increase transparency so everyone understands the work and progress.

    • Continuously improve the product and the team's process.

    • Reduce risk by getting feedback early and often.

    Example

    Imagine building an AI Pharmacovigilance Platform.

    • Traditional approach: Build all modules for 12 months, then release the full platform.

    • Scrum approach: Build Login and Dashboard in Sprint 1, get feedback, then continue with ICSR Management, AI Data Extraction, and MedDRA Coding in later Sprints.

    The Scrum approach reduces the risk of building features that users may not need or use.

    History of Scrum

    Origin of the term

    The term "Scrum" comes from the sport of rugby, where players work together closely to move the ball toward a goal.

    In rugby, a scrum is a formation where team members collaborate tightly, adjust to changing conditions, and work as a unit.

    Early inspiration

    In 1986, Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka published an article in Harvard Business Review titled "The New New Product Development Game".

    They observed that successful product development teams:

    • Worked collaboratively.

    • Moved quickly.

    • Adapted to changing conditions.

    • Developed products in overlapping phases rather than strict sequential stages.

    This article inspired the Scrum framework.

    Formal development of Scrum

    Scrum was formally developed in the early 1990s by:

    • Jeff Sutherland.

    • Ken Schwaber.

    They presented Scrum publicly in 1995 and later became co-authors of the Scrum Guide, which defines the official Scrum framework.

    Scrum timeline

    1986

    Takeuchi and Nonaka describe the "Scrum" concept in product development.

    1993

    Jeff Sutherland begins applying Scrum ideas in software development.

    1995

    Sutherland and Schwaber present Scrum publicly.

    2001

    Agile Manifesto is published, and Scrum becomes one of the most popular Agile frameworks.

    2010+

    Scrum becomes widely adopted across software, healthcare, finance, and product development industries.

    Key ideas behind Scrum

    Empirical process control

    Scrum is based on empirical process control, which means decisions are made based on observation, experience, and experimentation rather than assumptions.

    Scrum relies on three pillars:

    Pillar Meaning

    Transparency Everyone can see the work, progress, and challenges.

    Inspection The team regularly reviews the product and process.

    Adaptation The team adjusts plans and practices based on feedback and findings.

    Iterative and incremental development

    • Iterative means the team repeats cycles of planning, building, testing, and reviewing.

    • Incremental means each Sprint produces a usable addition to the product.

    Scrum values

    Scrum is guided by five core values:

    Value Meaning

    Commitment The team commits to achieving the Sprint Goal.

    Focus The team concentrates on the work selected for the Sprint.

    Openness The team is transparent about progress and challenges.

    Respect Team members value each other's contributions.

    Courage The team is willing to face difficult problems and make improvements.

    When Scrum should be used

    Scrum is most suitable when:

    • The project is complex and evolving.

    • Requirements are likely to change.

    • Customer or stakeholder feedback is important.

    • The product can be delivered in small increments.

    • The team can work collaboratively in short iterations.

    • Continuous improvement is desired.

    Examples of suitable projects

    • Software development projects.

    • AI and machine learning products.

    • Healthcare software systems.

    • Pharmacovigilance platforms.

    • Mobile and web applications.

    • Product innovation initiatives.

    When Scrum may not be the best choice

    Scrum may be less suitable when:

    • Requirements are fully fixed and unlikely to change.

    • The project involves physical construction or manufacturing.

    • Work is primarily continuous support or maintenance with unpredictable incoming tasks.

    • The organization cannot support cross-functional collaboration.

    • Stakeholders are not available for regular feedback.

    In such cases, Waterfall, Kanban, or a hybrid approach may be more appropriate.

    Advantages of Scrum

    • Faster delivery of value through frequent increments.

    • Better adaptability to changing requirements.

    • Improved collaboration between business and technical teams.

    • Higher transparency through regular events and visible backlog.

    • Continuous improvement through retrospectives.

    • Reduced project risk through early feedback and inspection.

    Limitations of Scrum

    • Requires active stakeholder involvement.

    • Requires cross-functional teams.

    • May feel too structured for some continuous-flow environments.

    • Can be difficult in organizations with rigid hierarchical structures.

    • Requires discipline in following Scrum roles and events.

    Common misconceptions

    • "Scrum is the same as Agile." Scrum is one Agile framework, not Agile itself.

    • "Scrum guarantees success." Scrum improves transparency and adaptability, but success depends on team collaboration and proper implementation.

    • "Scrum means no planning." Scrum involves continuous planning through backlog refinement and Sprint Planning.

    Best practices

    • Keep the Product Backlog prioritized and transparent.

    • Define a clear Sprint Goal for each Sprint.

    • Ensure the team is cross-functional.

    • Conduct regular Sprint Reviews with stakeholders.

    • Use Sprint Retrospectives to improve the team's process.

    • Deliver a usable product increment at the end of each Sprint.

    Q3. What is the purpose of Scrum?

    Answer: The purpose of Scrum is to help teams deliver valuable product increments quickly, adapt to changing requirements, improve collaboration, and continuously improve their process.

    Q4. When should Scrum be used?

    Answer: Scrum should be used for complex projects with evolving requirements, where incremental delivery, stakeholder feedback, and cross-functional collaboration are important.

    Overview of Scrum roles

    The Scrum Team is a small, self-managing, and cross-functional team responsible for delivering a valuable product increment each Sprint.

    According to the Scrum Guide, the Scrum Team consists of:

    • Product Owner -- Maximizes product value.

    • Scrum Master -- Facilitates Scrum and helps the team improve.

    • Developers -- Build the product increment.

    Product Owner

    Purpose

    The Product Owner (PO) is responsible for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team.

    Key responsibilities

    • Define and communicate the Product Goal.

    • Create and manage the Product Backlog.

    • Prioritize backlog items based on business value.

    • Ensure backlog items are clear and understood.

    • Collaborate with stakeholders to gather requirements and feedback.

    • Make decisions about what should be built next.

    Simple analogy

    The Product Owner is like a restaurant owner who decides:

    • What dishes should be on the menu.

    • Which dishes are most important to prepare first.

    • What customers value most.

    Healthcare example

    In a Hospital Management System, the Product Owner may prioritize:

    • Patient registration.

    • Appointment scheduling.

    • Prescription management.

    • Billing integration.

    Pharmacovigilance example

    For your AI Pharmacovigilance Platform, the Product Owner may prioritize:

    • ICSR Management.

    • AI Data Extraction.

    • MedDRA Coding.

    • Duplicate Detection.

    • Signal Detection.

    Common mistakes

    • Not prioritizing the backlog clearly.

    • Acting as a project manager instead of a value maximizer.

    • Ignoring stakeholder feedback.

    • Writing unclear backlog items.

    Scrum Master

    Purpose

    The Scrum Master is responsible for establishing Scrum and helping everyone understand and apply Scrum theory and practice.

    Key responsibilities

    • Facilitate Scrum events such as Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective.

    • Remove impediments that block the team's progress.

    • Coach the Scrum Team in Agile and Scrum practices.

    • Help the team become self-managing and cross-functional.

    • Promote continuous improvement.

    • Support collaboration between the Scrum Team and stakeholders.

    Simple analogy

    The Scrum Master is like a sports coach who:

    • Ensures the team follows the rules.

    • Removes obstacles affecting performance.

    • Helps team members improve their skills.

    • Encourages collaboration and discipline.

    Healthcare example

    In a Hospital Management System project, the Scrum Master may:

    • Facilitate discussions between developers and hospital staff.

    • Resolve blockers related to system integration.

    • Ensure Sprint ceremonies are effective.

    Pharmacovigilance example

    For the AI Pharmacovigilance Platform, the Scrum Master may:

    • Remove blockers related to AI model training.

    • Coordinate with regulatory experts.

    • Help the team improve Sprint delivery.

    Common mistakes

    • Acting as a manager who assigns tasks.

    • Ignoring impediments.

    • Focusing only on meetings instead of team improvement.

    • Not coaching the team in Scrum values and practices.

    Developers

    Purpose

    Developers are the members of the Scrum Team who are responsible for creating the product increment each Sprint.

    Key responsibilities

    • Plan the work needed to achieve the Sprint Goal.

    • Build, test, and integrate product features.

    • Maintain the quality of the product increment.

    • Collaborate with each other and with the Product Owner.

    • Manage their own work and commitments.

    • Inspect progress daily and adapt the Sprint plan as needed.

    Simple analogy

    Developers are like the chefs in a restaurant who prepare the dishes selected by the restaurant owner.

    Healthcare example

    In a Hospital Management System, Developers may include:

    • Frontend developers.

    • Backend developers.

    • Database engineers.

    • QA testers.

    • Integration specialists.

    Pharmacovigilance example

    For the AI Pharmacovigilance Platform, Developers may include:

    • Software developers.

    • AI/ML engineers.

    • Data engineers.

    • QA testers.

    • Pharmacovigilance domain experts contributing to validation.

    Common mistakes

    • Waiting for the Scrum Master to assign tasks.

    • Working in silos without collaboration.

    • Ignoring quality practices.

    • Not communicating progress or blockers.

    Stakeholders

    Purpose

    Stakeholders are individuals or groups who have an interest in the product and can influence or be affected by its outcome.

    Stakeholders are not part of the Scrum Team, but they play an important role by providing feedback, requirements, and business context.

    Examples of stakeholders

    • Customers.

    • End users.

    • Business managers.

    • Regulatory authorities.

    • Healthcare professionals.

    • Pharmacovigilance specialists.

    • Investors or sponsors.

    Role in Scrum

    • Provide feedback during Sprint Reviews.

    • Help clarify business needs and priorities.

    • Support product vision and adoption.

    • Raise concerns about compliance, usability, or business impact.

    Healthcare example

    Stakeholders for a Hospital Management System may include:

    • Doctors.

    • Nurses.

    • Hospital administrators.

    • Patients.

    • IT support staff.

    Pharmacovigilance example

    Stakeholders for the AI Pharmacovigilance Platform may include:

    • Pharmacovigilance officers.

    • Regulatory affairs specialists.

    • Drug safety managers.

    • Medical reviewers.

    • Compliance auditors.

    • Product Owner: Keep the Product Backlog clear, prioritized, and aligned with business value.

    • Developers: Collaborate closely, maintain quality, and take ownership of Sprint commitments.

    Interview questions

    Q1. What is the role of the Product Owner?

    Answer: The Product Owner is responsible for maximizing product value by managing and prioritizing the Product Backlog and ensuring that the team works on the most valuable items.

    Q2. What does the Scrum Master do?

    Answer: The Scrum Master facilitates Scrum events, removes impediments, coaches the team in Agile and Scrum practices, and helps the team improve continuously.

    Q3. Who are Developers in Scrum?

    Answer: Developers are the members of the Scrum Team responsible for creating the product increment during each Sprint.

    Q4. Are Stakeholders part of the Scrum Team?

    Lesson 17: Scrum Artifacts -- Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, and Increment

    Learning objectives

    Planning Poker

    Purpose

    Planning Poker is a collaborative estimation technique used by Scrum Teams to assign Story Points to backlog items.

    • The Product Owner explains a backlog item.

    • Team members discuss the requirements.

    • Each member privately selects a Story Point estimate.

    • All estimates are revealed simultaneously.

    • If estimates differ, the team discusses the reasons.

    • The team repeats the process until a consensus is reached.

    Benefits

    • Encourages team collaboration.

    • Reduces the influence of dominant personalities.

    • Surfaces hidden assumptions and risks.

    • Improves estimation accuracy.

    What is Kanban?

    Kanban is a workflow management method used to visualize work, limit work in progress, and improve the flow of tasks through a process.

    The word "Kanban" comes from Japanese and means "visual signal" or "signboard".

    Simple definition: Kanban is an Agile method that helps teams manage and improve their workflow by making work visible and limiting the amount of work being done at the same time.

    Purpose of Kanban

    Kanban was created to help teams:

    • Visualize the workflow.

    • Identify bottlenecks.

    • Reduce multitasking.

    • Improve flow efficiency.

    • Deliver work continuously.

    • Adapt to changing priorities.

    Simple analogy

    Imagine a pharmacy dispensing counter:

    • Prescriptions arrive continuously.

    • Each prescription moves through stages: Received → Verification → Dispensing → Completed.

    • If too many prescriptions are in Verification, it becomes a bottleneck.

    • Limiting the number of prescriptions in verification helps maintain smooth flow.

    This is similar to how Kanban manages work in software and product development.

    History of Kanban

    Kanban originated in the Toyota Production System in the 1940s and 1950s.

    • Toyota used visual cards to signal when more materials were needed.

    • This helped reduce waste and improve production flow.

    • Later, Kanban principles were adapted for knowledge work and software development by David J. Anderson.

    Core principles of Kanban

    1. Visualize the workflow

    Make all work visible so the team can understand:

    • What work is waiting.

    • What work is in progress.

    • What work is completed.

    2. Limit Work In Progress (WIP)

    WIP limits restrict the number of tasks allowed in a workflow stage at one time.

    • Prevents overloading the team.

    • Reduces context switching.

    • Highlights bottlenecks quickly.

    3. Manage flow

    Focus on moving work smoothly from start to completion.

    • Reduce delays.

    • Remove blockers.

    • Improve cycle time.

    4. Make policies explicit

    Clearly define:

    • How work enters the system.

    • What each workflow stage means.

    • When work is considered complete.

    5. Implement feedback loops

    Regularly review workflow performance and identify improvement opportunities.

    6. Improve collaboratively and evolve experimentally

    Kanban encourages teams to make small, continuous improvements rather than large disruptive changes.

    1. What is Lean?

    Simple definition

    Lean is a methodology focused on maximizing customer value while minimizing waste.

    In simple words:

    Build what customers need, remove unnecessary work, and continuously improve the process.

    Simple analogy

    Imagine a hospital emergency department.

    A patient arrives.

    The process:

    Now imagine:

    • Patient waits 2 hours for registration.

    • Same information is entered three times.

    • Doctors wait for reports.

    • Staff perform unnecessary paperwork.

    Lean thinking asks:

    \"Which steps actually create value for the patient, and which steps are waste?\"

    2. Why was Lean created?

    Lean originated from the Toyota Production System (TPS).

    After World War II, Japan had:

    • Limited resources.

    • Limited money.

    • High competition.

    Toyota needed a way to:

    • Produce high-quality products.

    • Reduce waste.

    • Use resources efficiently.

    Toyota developed a system focused on:

    • Continuous improvement.

    • Employee involvement.

    • Eliminating unnecessary activities.

    This became known globally as Lean Manufacturing.

    Later, Lean concepts moved into:

    • Software development.

    • Healthcare.

    • Product management.

    • Startups.

    • AI development.

    1. What is Extreme Programming (XP)?

    Simple definition

    Extreme Programming (XP) is an Agile software development framework focused on delivering high-quality software through strong engineering practices, continuous feedback, and frequent releases.

    In simple words:

    Scrum helps teams decide what to build and when to build it. XP focuses on building it correctly with high technical quality.

    Simple analogy

    Imagine building a hospital.

    Scrum answers:

    • Which departments should be built first?

    • What should be delivered in the next phase?

    XP answers:

    • Are the electrical systems safe?

    • Are materials high quality?

    • Are construction standards followed?

    Both focus on successful delivery, but XP emphasizes quality of construction.

    2. Why was XP created?

    In traditional software development:

    Teams often faced problems:

    • Requirements changed frequently.

    • Bugs appeared late.

    • Testing happened at the end.

    • Developers worked separately.

    • Releases were slow.

    XP was created to solve these problems by introducing:

    • Continuous testing.

    • Frequent releases.

    • Close collaboration.

    • Better coding practices.

    3. History of Extreme Programming

    XP was created by:

    Kent Beck

    during the late 1990s.

    It was first used in a project called:

    Chrysler Comprehensive Compensation System (C3 Project)

    The project faced problems with traditional development approaches.

    Kent Beck introduced XP practices to:

    • Improve software quality.

    • Increase developer collaboration.

    • Deliver faster.

    The book:

    \"Extreme Programming Explained\"

    was published by Kent Beck in 1999.

    The idea:

    Take good software development practices and apply them to the extreme.

    Examples:

    \"Test software.\"

    \"Write tests continuously before coding.\"

    Normal practice:

    \"Review code.\"

    XP:

    \"Two developers work together continuously.\"

    Normal practice:

    \"Integrate changes.\"

    XP:

    \"Integrate code multiple times every day.\"

    1. What is DSDM?

    Simple definition

    Dynamic Systems Development Method (DSDM) is an Agile framework that focuses on delivering business solutions quickly while maintaining strong governance, collaboration, and quality.

    Simple meaning:

    DSDM helps organizations build the right solution, at the right time, with strong business involvement.

    Simple analogy

    Imagine a pharmaceutical company building a:

    Drug Safety Management System

    Many people are involved:

    • Pharmacovigilance team.

    • Regulatory department.

    • IT team.

    • Quality assurance.

    • Compliance team.

    • Management.

    A simple development approach may fail because:

    • Requirements are complex.

    • Regulations are strict.

    • Many stakeholders are involved.

    DSDM provides:

    • Clear roles.

    • Strong communication.

    • Prioritization.

    • Governance.

    2. Why was DSDM created?

    Before Agile:

    Organizations faced problems:

    • Projects took years.

    • Requirements changed.

    • Users received software too late.

    • Business teams were disconnected from developers.

    However, some organizations also needed:

    • More structure.

    • More governance.

    • More predictability.

    DSDM was created to combine:

    Agile flexibility + business discipline

    3. History of DSDM

    DSDM was created in:

    1994

    by a consortium of organizations in the UK.

    The goal:

    Create a standard Agile approach for Rapid Application Development (RAD).

    It became one of the earliest Agile frameworks.

    Later:

    DSDM became known as:

    Agile Project Framework

    4. Core philosophy of DSDM

    The main idea:

    \"Time and resources are fixed, but scope can change.\"

    This is opposite to traditional projects where:

    Fixed:

    • Scope.

    Flexible:

    • Time and cost.

    Traditional Waterfall

    Fixed scope

    ↓

    Estimate time

    ↓

    Estimate cost

    DSDM

    Fixed:

    • Time.

    • Cost.

    • Quality.

    Flexible:

    • Features.

    Example:

    A healthcare app must launch in 3 months.

    Instead of delaying launch:

    DSDM says:

    Deliver the most valuable features first.

    Move less important features later.

    5. DSDM Principles

    DSDM has eight principles.

    Principle 1: Focus on Business Need

    Everything should deliver business value.

    Question:

    \"Why are we building this?\"

    Example:

    \"It reduces manual pharmacovigilance processing time.\"

    Principle 2: Deliver on Time

    Time-boxing is critical.

    Example:

    Release must happen on:

    Features are adjusted to meet the deadline.

    Principle 3: Collaborate

    Business and technical teams work together.

    Fast delivery does not mean poor quality.

    Deliver in small pieces.

    Example:

    AI extraction.

    Version 3:

    Signal detection.

    Principle 6: Develop Iteratively

    Build → Test → Feedback → Improve.

    Principle 7: Demonstrate Control

    Maintain:

    • Visibility.

    • Governance.

    • Tracking.

    Principle 8: Communicate Continuously

    Frequent communication prevents misunderstanding.

    Interview Questions

    Q1. What is Spotify Model?

    Answer:

    Spotify Model is an organizational approach that uses autonomous cross-functional teams called squads supported by tribes, chapters, and guilds.

    Q2. What is a Squad?

    Answer:

    A squad is a small autonomous cross-functional team responsible for a specific product area.

    Q3. Difference between Chapter and Guild?

    Answer:

    A Chapter is a group of people with the same expertise inside an organization, while a Guild is a voluntary community sharing knowledge and interests.

    Q4. Is Spotify Model a framework?

    Answer:

    No. It is an organizational model, not a formal Agile framework.
