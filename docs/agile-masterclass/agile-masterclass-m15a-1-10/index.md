# Agile Masterclass_M15A (1-10)

**Lesson 1: Business Case & Feasibility Analysis (Masterclass)**

**1. Why This Topic Matters for a Business Analyst**

Many beginners think:

\"BA receives requirements and writes documents.\"

That is a **junior BA mindset**.

Industry BA works earlier:

**Business Problem → Opportunity → Business Case → Feasibility →
Requirements → Solution**

Example:

A pharmaceutical company says:

\"We want an AI system to detect adverse drug reactions automatically.\"

A junior BA asks:

\"What fields should the form contain?\"

A senior BA asks:

\"Why do we need this system? What business problem exists? What value
will AI create? Is it feasible?\"

**2. What is a Business Case?**

A **Business Case** is a document that explains:

\"Why should the organization invest money, time, and resources into
this initiative?\"

It answers:

-   Why are we doing this?

-   What problem are we solving?

-   What benefits will we get?

-   How much will it cost?

-   What risks exist?

-   Should we proceed or not?

**Simple Formula**

Business Case

=

Problem

\+

Opportunity

\+

Solution Options

\+

Cost

\+

Benefits

\+

Risk

\+

Recommendation

**3. Business Case vs Requirement Document**

Many BA beginners confuse these.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Business Case**                     **Requirement Document**
  ------------------------------------- ---------------------------------
  Explains WHY                          Explains WHAT

  Created before project approval       Created after approval

  Business focused                      Solution focused

  Used by executives                    Used by project teams

  Contains ROI                          Contains features/functions
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example:

**Business Case**

Problem:

Pharmacovigilance team manually reviews 50,000 safety reports monthly.

Impact:

-   30 days processing time

-   High operational cost

-   Risk of delayed reporting

Proposal:

AI-powered adverse event detection platform

Expected benefit:

-   Reduce review time by 70%

-   Improve compliance

**Requirement Document**

Functional requirement:

System shall classify adverse event reports into seriousness categories.

**4. Components of a Business Case**

A professional BA business case usually contains:

**Section 1: Executive Summary**

One-page overview.

Example:

Project:

AI Pharmacovigilance Intelligence Platform

Objective:

Reduce manual adverse event review using AI.

Expected Outcome:

Decrease processing time from 30 days to 10 days.

**Section 2: Business Problem Statement**

A good BA defines the problem clearly.

Bad:

\"We need AI.\"

Good:

\"The pharmacovigilance team currently spends 60% of its time manually
reviewing incoming safety reports, causing delays in signal detection.\"

**Problem Statement Template**

Currently:

Who is affected?

What problem exists?

How often does it occur?

What is the business impact?

Why solve now?

  ------------------ ----------------------------------------------------
  Option 1           Hire more analysts

**Section 6: Feasibility Analysis**

This is a major BA responsibility.

There are five feasibility types.

Remember:

**TELOS Framework**

T - Technical

E - Economic

L - Legal

O - Operational

S - Schedule

**1. Technical Feasibility**

Question:

Can we build this technology?

Example:

AI Pharmacovigilance System:

Check:

-   Data availability

-   AI model capability

-   Cloud infrastructure

-   Integration capability

**2. Economic Feasibility**

Question:

Does benefit justify cost?

Example:

Cost:

AI Development

\$300,000

Annual Maintenance

\$50,000

Benefits:

Reduced manual effort

\$250,000/year

Reduced compliance risk

\$150,000/year

ROI calculation:

ROI =

(Benefit - Cost)

\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\--

Cost

**3. Legal Feasibility**

Especially important in healthcare.

Questions:

-   GDPR compliance?

-   HIPAA?

-   Patient privacy?

-   Data retention rules?

-   Regulatory requirements?

**4. Operational Feasibility**

Question:

Will users actually adopt it?

Example:

Safety associates may resist AI.

BA checks:

-   Training needs

-   Process changes

-   User acceptance

**5. Schedule Feasibility**

Question:

Can we deliver within required timeline?

Example:

Regulatory deadline:

6 months

Development estimate:

12 months

Project not feasible.

**7. Cost-Benefit Analysis**

BA compares:

**Costs**

**Direct Cost**

-   Development

-   Software licenses

-   Cloud cost

-   Training

**Indirect Cost**

-   Process change

-   Migration

-   Maintenance

**Benefits**

**Tangible Benefits**

Can be measured.

Example:

-   Save employee hours

-   Reduce operational cost

**Intangible Benefits**

Harder to measure.

Example:

-   Better customer trust

-   Improved compliance reputation

**Lesson 2: Requirement Prioritization Masterclass**

**1. Why Requirement Prioritization Matters for a BA**

In real projects, stakeholders rarely give 5--10 requirements.

They give **hundreds**.

Example:

A pharmaceutical company wants an **AI Pharmacovigilance Platform**.

Stakeholders request:

-   AI adverse event extraction

-   Mobile app

-   Dashboard

-   Voice reporting

-   Multiple language support

-   Blockchain audit trail

-   Chatbot

-   Automated regulatory submission

-   Patient portal

-   Predictive safety signals

But:

-   Budget is limited

-   Timeline is limited

-   Team capacity is limited

The BA must answer:

\"Which requirements should we build first?\"

This is **Requirement Prioritization**.

**2. What is Requirement Prioritization?**

Requirement prioritization is the process of:

Evaluating requirements based on business value, urgency, risk, cost,
and feasibility to decide implementation order.

**Simple Formula**

Priority

=

Business Value

\+

Urgency

\+

Risk Reduction

\-

Cost

\-

Complexity

A BA does not prioritize alone.

Usually:

\|

\|

Different stakeholders bring different perspectives.

**4. Factors Used for Prioritization**

A BA evaluates:

**1. Business Value**

Question:

How much value does this provide?

Example:

Automatic adverse event classification:

High value.

**2. User Impact**

Question:

How many users benefit?

Example:

Dashboard used by 500 safety analysts.

High impact.

**3. Regulatory Importance**

Important in healthcare.

Example:

FDA reporting compliance.

High priority.

**4. Risk Reduction**

Question:

Does this reduce business risk?

Example:

AI duplicate case detection.

Reduces compliance risk.

**5. Cost**

Question:

How expensive is implementation?

**6. Complexity**

Question:

How difficult is it?

**5. Common Requirement Prioritization Techniques**

A professional BA should know multiple techniques.

**Technique 1: MoSCoW Prioritization**

Most commonly used in Agile.

MoSCoW means:

M - Must Have

S - Should Have

C - Could Have

W - Won\'t Have Now

**A) Must Have**

Critical requirements.

Without this, product fails.

Example:

AI Pharmacovigilance MVP:

✅ Case ingestion\
✅ Adverse event extraction\
✅ User authentication\
✅ Audit logging

**B) Should Have**

Important but not critical.

Example:

-   Advanced analytics dashboard

-   Export reports

**C) Could Have**

Nice to have.

Example:

-   Mobile app

-   Voice commands

**D) Won\'t Have Now**

Deferred.

Example:

-   Blockchain integration

**MoSCoW Example**

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Requirement**                                **Priority**
  ---------------------------------------------- ------------------------
  Login                                          Must

  ADR extraction                                 Must

  Dashboard                                      Should

  Mobile App                                     Could

  Blockchain                                     Won\'t
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

**Technique 3: Weighted Scoring Model**

Used in enterprise organizations.

BA assigns scores.

Example criteria:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Criteria**                                   **Weight**
  ---------------------------------------------- ------------------------
  Business Value                                 40%

  User Impact                                    25%

  Risk Reduction                                 20%

  Complexity                                     15%
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Factor**                                         **Score**
  -------------------------------------------------- --------------------
  Business Value                                     9

  User Impact                                        8

  Risk Reduction                                     10

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Calculation:

Priority Score =

(Value × Weight)

\+

(Impact × Weight)

\+

(Risk × Weight)

\-

(Complexity × Weight)

Highest score gets priority.

**2. Performance Needs**

\|

**Technique 6: WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First)**

Used in SAFe Agile.

Formula:

WSJF =

Cost of Delay

\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\-\--

Job Size

Where:

Cost of Delay:

Business Value

\+

Time Criticality

\+

Risk Reduction

Example:

Feature A:

Business Value = 8

Time Criticality = 7

Risk Reduction = 9

Job Size = 4

Cost of Delay:

8+7+9=24

WSJF:

24/4 = 6

Higher WSJF = higher priority.

**Lesson 3: Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM) Masterclass**

**1. Why RTM is Important for a Business Analyst**

In real projects, requirements change continuously.

Example:

A pharmaceutical company builds an **AI Pharmacovigilance System**.

Initial requirement:

\"System should automatically classify adverse events.\"

Later:

Regulatory team says:

\"System must also maintain an audit trail showing why AI made each
classification.\"

Now questions arise:

-   Was this requirement captured?

-   Was it developed?

-   Was it tested?

-   Did it meet compliance needs?

-   Who approved the change?

This is where **RTM** helps.

**2. What is Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM)?**

A Requirement Traceability Matrix is a document that connects:

Business Requirements → Functional Requirements → User Stories →
Development Tasks → Test Cases → Deployment

It ensures every requirement is:

✅ Identified\
✅ Implemented\
✅ Tested\
✅ Approved

**Simple Definition**

RTM is a tracking document that proves that every business requirement
has been delivered and validated.

**4. Why Organizations Use RTM**

**1. Prevent Missing Requirements**

**Lesson 4: Business Rules & Decision Modeling Masterclass**

**1. Why Business Rules Matter for a BA**

A modern system does not only store data or perform actions.

It makes **decisions**.

Examples:

A banking system decides:

-   Approve or reject loan?

-   Calculate interest rate?

-   Detect fraud?

A healthcare system decides:

-   Is an adverse event serious?

-   Does a case require expedited reporting?

-   Should a safety signal be escalated?

These decisions are controlled by **Business Rules**.

**2. What is a Business Rule?**

A business rule is a statement that defines:

\"How an organization makes decisions, operates, or enforces policies.\"

**Simple Examples**

**Banking**

IF customer credit score \> 750

THEN loan eligibility = High

**Insurance**

IF claim amount \> ₹5 lakh

THEN manager approval required

**Pharmacovigilance**

IF adverse event = Serious

AND patient outcome = Death

THEN expedited regulatory reporting required

**3. Business Rule vs Requirement**

Many BAs confuse these.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Business Rule**                 **Requirement**
  --------------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Defines decision logic            Defines system capability

  Business policy                   System behavior

  \"What should happen?\"           \"What should system do?\"
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example:

**Business Rule**

Serious adverse events must be reported within 15 days.

**Requirement**

System shall automatically identify serious adverse events and generate
regulatory reports.

**4. Characteristics of Good Business Rules**

A good business rule should be:

**1. Clear**

Bad:

High-risk cases should be reviewed quickly.

Good:

Cases classified as critical must be reviewed within 24 hours.

**2. Atomic**

One rule = one decision.

Bad:

If patient is old and drug is risky and symptoms are severe,

send report and notify doctor and update database.

Too many rules combined.

**3. Testable**

A QA team should validate it.

**4. Traceable**

Connected to:

-   Policy

-   Regulation

-   Requirement

-   Test case

**5. Sources of Business Rules**

BA discovers rules from:

**1. Regulations**

Example:

**2. Company Policies**

Example:

Internal safety review process.

**3. Subject Matter Experts**

Example:

Medical experts.

**4. Existing Systems**

Example:

Legacy application logic.

**5. Contracts**

Example:

Insurance agreements.

**2. What is a Use Case?**

**Lesson 9: BA Metrics & KPI Framework (Masterclass)**

**Goal:** Learn how a Business Analyst defines, measures, tracks, and

**Learning Objectives**

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

-   Understand Metrics, KPIs, KRIs, and OKRs

-   Differentiate leading and lagging indicators

-   Measure business, project, product, and BA performance

-   Design executive dashboards

-   Define AI and healthcare KPIs

-   Measure ROI after implementation

-   Answer KPI-related interview questions

**1. Why Metrics Matter**

Imagine two Business Analysts complete the same project.

**BA 1 says:**

\"The system has been delivered successfully.\"

**BA 2 says:**

\"The system reduced manual processing time by 62%, increased user
productivity by 38%, achieved 97% user adoption within three months, and
reduced compliance errors by 81%.\"

Which BA demonstrates more business value?

The second one.

Modern organizations want **measurable outcomes**, not just completed
documentation.

**2. What is a Metric?**

A **Metric** is any measurable value.

Examples:

-   Number of users

-   Processing time

-   Revenue

-   Number of defects

-   Number of safety cases

-   API response time

Metrics describe performance but don\'t necessarily indicate strategic
success.

**3. What is a KPI?**

A **Key Performance Indicator (KPI)** is a metric that measures progress
toward an important business objective.

Example:

Metric:

Average review time = 18 minutes

KPI:

Reduce average review time to under 15 minutes by the end of Q4.

Every KPI is a metric, but not every metric is a KPI.

**4. KPI vs Metric vs KRI vs OKR**

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Term**                     **Purpose**
  ---------------------------- ------------------------------------------
  Metric                       Measures something

  KPI                          Measures success against a business
                               objective

  KRI (Key Risk Indicator)     Warns about increasing risk

  OKR (Objectives & Key        Goal-setting framework
  Results)                     
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example:

Objective:

Improve pharmacovigilance efficiency.

Key Results:

-   Reduce manual review time by 40%

-   Increase AI accuracy to 95%

-   Reduce duplicate reports by 50%

KPIs are then tracked to determine whether these key results are being
achieved.

**5. Characteristics of Good KPIs (SMART)**

KPIs should be:

-   **Specific**

-   **Measurable**

-   **Achievable**

-   **Relevant**

-   **Time-bound**

Poor KPI:

Improve AI performance.

Better KPI:

Increase AI classification accuracy from 90% to 95% within six months.

**6. Types of Business Metrics**

Measure business outcomes.

-   Revenue

Examples:

-   Processing time

-   Cases handled per analyst

-   SLA compliance

**C. Product Metrics**

Measure product performance.

Examples:

-   User retention

**D. Project Metrics**

Measure project execution.

Examples:

-   Schedule variance

-   Budget variance

-   Defect rate

-   Sprint velocity

**Goal:** Learn how a Business Analyst plans, facilitates, and leads

**1. Why Workshop Facilitation is Important**

Imagine this situation:

A hospital wants to build an **AI Pharmacovigilance Platform**.

Participants include:

-   Medical Director

-   Safety Manager

-   Regulatory Affairs

-   Product Owner

-   Developers

-   QA Team

-   AI Engineers

-   UX Designer

Everyone has different expectations.

Without proper facilitation:

-   Meetings become discussions without decisions.

-   Requirements conflict.

-   Scope grows uncontrollably.

-   Stakeholders leave frustrated.

A skilled BA keeps the workshop focused and productive.

**2. What is a BA Workshop?**

A BA Workshop is:

**A structured meeting where stakeholders collaborate to discover,
analyze, prioritize, validate, or approve business requirements and
solutions.**

Unlike a status meeting, a workshop has a clear objective and expected
outputs.

**3. Why Use Workshops?**

Compared to separate interviews, workshops:

✅ Collect multiple viewpoints simultaneously

✅ Resolve conflicts quickly

✅ Build shared understanding

✅ Speed up decision-making

✅ Increase stakeholder ownership

**8. Facilitation Skills**

A facilitator guides discussion without dominating it.

Key skills include:

-   Active listening

-   Asking open-ended questions

-   Summarizing discussions

-   Encouraging participation

-   Managing time

-   Remaining neutral

-   Resolving conflicts

The facilitator\'s job is to help the group reach understanding and
decisions.

**9. Asking Effective Questions**

Avoid:

\"Do you like this feature?\"

Better:

\"How do you currently process serious adverse events?\"

Even better:

\"Walk me through the steps from receiving a safety report to regulatory
submission.\"

Open-ended questions reveal richer information.

**10. Stakeholder Management During Workshops**

Different personalities require different approaches.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Stakeholder Type**       **BA Approach**
  -------------------------- --------------------------------------------
  Quiet participant          Invite their opinion directly

  Dominant speaker           Acknowledge and redirect discussion

  Subject Matter Expert      Validate detailed business rules

  Executive                  Focus on outcomes and decisions

  Technical Lead             Clarify feasibility and constraints

  Skeptical stakeholder      Explore concerns with evidence
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The goal is balanced participation.

**11. Managing Conflicts**

Conflict is normal.

Example:

Regulatory Team:

\"We need every AI recommendation manually reviewed.\"

Product Team:

\"Manual review slows everything down.\"

The BA should:

-   Clarify the underlying business need.

-   Identify regulatory constraints.

-   Explore alternatives.

-   Evaluate risks.

-   Facilitate agreement.

Focus on solving the problem, not \"winning\" the argument.

**12. Common Facilitation Techniques**

**Brainstorming**

Generate ideas without immediate evaluation.

**Affinity Mapping**

Group similar ideas into themes.

Example:

Ideas:

-   Faster processing

-   Better dashboards

-   AI suggestions

-   Mobile access

Themes:

-   Automation

-   Reporting

-   User Experience

**MoSCoW Prioritization**

Rank requirements:

-   Must

-   Should

-   Could

-   Won\'t

**Dot Voting**

Each participant receives votes to prioritize ideas.

Useful when many options exist.

**Five Whys**

Identify root causes.

Problem:

Late regulatory submissions.

Why?

Manual review delays.

Why?

Too many duplicate reports.

Continue asking \"Why?\" until the underlying cause is identified.
