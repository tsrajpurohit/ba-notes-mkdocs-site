**Module 1 -- Introduction to Agile**

**Lesson 1: What is Agile?**

**1. Concept**

**Agile is a mindset and a way of working that helps teams build
products by delivering small pieces frequently, learning from feedback,
and continuously improving.**

Many beginners think Agile is:

❌ A software development process

❌ Scrum

❌ Jira

❌ Daily Standup meetings

These are **not** Agile.

Agile is **a philosophy**.

Everything else (Scrum, Kanban, Jira, Sprint, User Stories) comes later.

Think of Agile as the **operating system** of product development.

**2. Why Agile Exists**

Before understanding Agile\...

You first need to understand the problem.

Imagine you want to build a house.

**Traditional Method**

You spend six months creating drawings.

Then another six months constructing.

After one year\...

Customer says:

\"I don\'t like the kitchen.\"

Too late.

Everything is already built.

Huge money wasted.

Now imagine building room by room.

After building the kitchen\...

Customer says:

\"I want a bigger window.\"

You change it immediately.

Then build the bedroom.

Again get feedback.

Then bathroom.

Again improve.

This is Agile.

Instead of building everything first\...

You build\...

Learn\...

Improve\...

Repeat.

**3. Simple Explanation**

Imagine you\'re cooking for guests.

**Traditional Way**

Cook 25 dishes.

Guests arrive.

Nobody likes spicy food.

Everything is wasted.

**Agile Way**

Prepare one dish.

Serve.

Ask feedback.

Improve.

Cook next dish.

Again improve.

Eventually every dish matches guests\' tastes.

Agile works exactly like this.

**4. Real-life Analogy**

Imagine writing a book.

**Waterfall**

Write all 500 pages.

Then show publisher.

Publisher says:

\"Wrong audience.\"

Start over.

**Agile**

Write first chapter.

Publisher reviews.

Improve.

Write second chapter.

Improve.

Continue.

Much safer.

Agile accepts change instead of fighting it.

That is Agile\'s biggest strength.

**Module 1 -- Introduction to Agile**

**Lesson 2: Why Agile Was Created**

**1. Concept**

Agile was created because **traditional software development was failing
to deliver what businesses and customers actually needed**.

The problem wasn\'t that developers couldn\'t write code. The problem
was that **the world changes faster than long projects can keep up**.

Imagine you\'re driving to a destination using a map printed 10 years
ago. Roads have changed, traffic patterns have changed, and new highways
exist. Even if you follow the map perfectly, you may still end up in the
wrong place.

Traditional development assumed the \"map\" (requirements) would never
change.

Agile accepts that the map will change---and plans for it.

**2. Why It Exists**

Before Agile, most software projects followed a sequential model (later
known as Waterfall):

Requirements\
↓\
Design\
↓\
Development\
↓\
Testing\
↓\
Deployment

Each stage had to finish before the next one began.

This looked organized---but it created serious problems.

For example:

-   Requirements written in January

-   Development started in March

-   Testing in October

-   Product launched next January

By launch, customer needs might already have changed.

**3. Simple Explanation**

Imagine you\'re tailoring a suit.

**Traditional Approach**

-   Customer gives measurements.

-   Tailor works for two months.

-   Suit is delivered.

-   Customer has lost 8 kg.

The suit no longer fits.

**Agile Approach**

The tailor schedules fittings every week.

Week 1: Check measurements.

Week 2: Adjust sleeves.

Week 3: Adjust shoulders.

Week 4: Final fitting.

The finished suit fits perfectly because adjustments were made
throughout the process.

That\'s Agile.

**4. Real-Life Analogy**

Suppose you open a restaurant.

Traditional thinking:

-   Design a menu with 200 dishes.

-   Spend ₹20 lakh on equipment.

-   Hire staff.

-   Open after one year.

Customers arrive and only order 20 dishes.

Most of your investment was unnecessary.

Agile thinking:

Week 1:

-   Launch with 15 dishes.

Collect customer feedback.

Week 3:

-   Add popular items.

Week 6:

-   Remove dishes nobody orders.

Your menu evolves based on real customer behavior.

**5. Technical Explanation**

During the 1980s and 1990s, software projects became increasingly large
and complex.

Common issues included:

**1. Changing Requirements**

Businesses frequently changed their priorities while projects were still
underway.

Example:

A bank initially requests online fund transfers.

Six months later, regulations require two-factor authentication.

The original requirements are now incomplete.

**2. Late Customer Feedback**

Customers often didn\'t see the software until it was almost finished.

By then, changing it was expensive and time-consuming.

**3. High Failure Rates**

Many projects experienced:

-   Budget overruns

-   Schedule delays

-   Missing features

-   Low customer satisfaction

**4. Poor Communication**

Business teams wrote long requirement documents.

Developers interpreted them.

Testers interpreted them again.

Important details were often misunderstood.

**5. High Cost of Change**

In traditional development:

Requirement Change\
\
↓\
\
Rewrite Design\
\
↓\
\
Rewrite Code\
\
↓\
\
Retest Everything\
\
↓\
\
Delay Project\
\
↓\
\
Increase Cost

The later a change happened, the more expensive it became.

Agile reduces this risk by encouraging small, frequent adjustments.

**9. Common Mistakes**

**❌ \"Agile was created to make development faster.\"**

Not exactly.

Its primary goal is **to deliver the right product**, not merely to
increase speed.

**❌ \"Planning isn\'t important in Agile.\"**

Agile still plans---but planning happens continuously rather than only
once at the beginning.

**❌ \"Requirements don\'t matter.\"**

Requirements are essential.

Agile simply recognizes that they may evolve.

**❌ \"Change means poor planning.\"**

Not always.

Markets, regulations, customer expectations, and technology all change.

Agile prepares teams to respond effectively.

**10. Best Practices**

-   Expect requirements to evolve.

-   Deliver usable software early.

-   Involve customers regularly.

-   Break large projects into smaller increments.

-   Learn from every release.

-   Improve continuously based on evidence.

**Lesson 3: Problems with Traditional Waterfall**

**Learning Objective**

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to answer:

-   What is Waterfall?

-   Why was Waterfall successful for many years?

-   Why does it struggle in software development?

-   When should Waterfall still be used?

-   How does Agile solve Waterfall\'s biggest problems?

**1. Concept**

Before Agile existed, almost every software project followed the
**Waterfall Model**.

The name \"Waterfall\" comes from the idea that work flows downward,
like a waterfall.

Requirements\
↓\
Design\
↓\
Development\
↓\
Testing\
↓\
Deployment\
↓\
Maintenance

Each phase must be completed before moving to the next.

You **do not go back** unless absolutely necessary.

Think of climbing down a waterfall---you can\'t easily climb back up.

**2. Why Waterfall Was Created**

In the 1960s and 1970s, software was:

-   Smaller

-   Less complex

-   Built for governments and large organizations

-   Used on mainframe computers

-   Rarely changed after deployment

Projects needed:

-   Heavy planning

-   Detailed documentation

-   Strict approvals

Waterfall worked well because requirements changed very little.

**3. Simple Explanation**

Imagine building a bridge.

Before construction begins, engineers know:

-   Length

-   Height

-   Materials

-   Budget

-   Safety standards

You cannot build half a bridge and ask:

\"Would you like it longer?\"

Everything must be planned first.

Waterfall is perfect here.

Now imagine building a social media app.

You don\'t know:

-   Which features users will love

-   What competitors will launch next month

-   What regulations will change

-   How user behavior will evolve

This uncertainty makes Waterfall much less suitable.

**4. Real-Life Analogy**

Imagine ordering a custom wedding cake.

**Waterfall**

You choose:

-   Flavor

-   Size

-   Design

-   Decoration

The baker spends two weeks making it.

When you see it:

\"Actually, I\'d like it to be chocolate instead of vanilla.\"

The baker has to start over.

**Agile**

The baker first creates a small sample.

You taste it.

Adjust sweetness.

Try frosting.

Choose decorations.

The final cake matches your expectations because you gave feedback
throughout.

**5. Technical Explanation**

Waterfall follows a **linear sequential model**.

Phase 1 → Phase 2 → Phase 3 → Phase 4

Each phase depends on the previous one being completed.

For example:

Requirements\
↓\
System Design\
↓\
Coding\
↓\
Testing\
↓\
Release

Testing only begins after coding is complete.

If testers discover a major issue, developers may need to revisit
earlier work, causing delays and increased costs.

**6. Why Waterfall Worked in Other Industries**

Waterfall is still excellent when requirements are stable.

Examples:

**Building a Hospital**

You cannot:

-   Build ICU first

-   Design the foundation later

Everything must be planned.

**Manufacturing an Aircraft**

Changing the wing design after production starts could be dangerous and
extremely expensive.

**Highway Construction**

Road dimensions, bridges, and materials are determined before
construction.

Frequent changes would waste enormous amounts of money.

**7. Why Waterfall Fails in Software**

Software is different because it is easy to change technically---but
difficult to change late in a project if everything depends on earlier
decisions.

Here are the biggest challenges.

**Problem 1 -- Requirements Change**

Month 1:

Customer wants Feature A.

Month 8:

Customer now needs Feature B instead.

Waterfall assumes requirements remain fixed.

Reality rarely works that way.

**Problem 2 -- Late Feedback**

Customers may not see the product for many months.

By then, they often discover that the solution doesn\'t meet their real
needs.

**Problem 3 -- Expensive Changes**

Imagine:

100,000 lines of code written\
\
↓\
\
Customer requests a major workflow change\
\
↓\
\
Architecture changes\
\
↓\
\
Code rewritten\
\
↓\
\
Testing repeated\
\
↓\
\
Documentation updated

Late changes affect the entire project.

**Problem 4 -- Long Delivery Cycles**

Customers wait many months before receiving anything usable.

If the product is wrong, they only find out after significant time and
investment.

**Problem 5 -- High Risk**

If a project lasts two years:

-   Technology may change.

-   Competitors may release better products.

-   Business priorities may shift.

-   Regulations may evolve.

The final product can already be outdated.

**12. Common Mistakes**

❌ \"Waterfall is bad.\"

No.

Waterfall is an excellent model when:

-   Requirements are stable.

-   Changes are unlikely.

-   Safety and regulatory approvals require detailed upfront planning.

❌ \"Agile replaces Waterfall everywhere.\"

No.

Many organizations use a combination of both approaches, depending on
the project.

❌ \"Waterfall has no documentation.\"

Incorrect.

Waterfall is known for extensive documentation and formal approvals.

**13. Best Practices**

Choose Waterfall when:

-   Requirements are clear and stable.

-   Regulatory compliance demands fixed documentation.

-   Physical construction or manufacturing is involved.

Choose Agile when:

-   Requirements are expected to evolve.

-   Customer feedback is essential.

-   Innovation and rapid learning are priorities.

**14. Interview Questions**

**Q1. What is the Waterfall model?**

A sequential project management approach in which each phase is
completed before the next begins.

**Q2. What are Waterfall\'s biggest disadvantages?**

-   Difficulty handling changing requirements

-   Late customer feedback

-   High cost of change

-   Long delivery cycles

**Q3. Is Waterfall still useful?**

Yes. It is well suited for projects with stable requirements, such as
construction, infrastructure, aerospace, and some regulated engineering
projects.

**Q4. Why is Agile often preferred for software?**

Because software requirements frequently change, and Agile allows teams
to adapt through short iterations and continuous feedback.

**1. Concept**

The **Agile Manifesto** is a short document created in **2001** that
defines the core values of Agile software development.

It does **not** tell you:

-   How to run meetings

-   How long a Sprint should be

-   How to write User Stories

-   Which tool to use

Instead, it answers a more fundamental question:

**\"What should we value when building software?\"**

That is why it\'s called a **manifesto**, not a process manual.

**2. Why It Exists**

By the late 1990s, many software teams were frustrated.

Projects often had:

-   Hundreds of pages of documentation

-   Months of planning

-   Long approval chains

-   Very little customer interaction

-   Software delivered too late

The process became more important than the product.

Developers began asking:

\"Why are we spending more time writing documents than solving customer
problems?\"

**3. The Story Behind the Agile Manifesto**

In February 2001, **17 experienced software practitioners** met at a ski
resort in Utah, USA.

Although they used different development methods, they agreed on one
thing:

Traditional software projects focused too much on rigid processes and
not enough on delivering value.

Together they created the **Agile Manifesto**, a one-page document that
transformed software development.

**4. The Agile Manifesto**

The Agile Manifesto says:

**We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and
helping others do it.**

It continues:

Through this work we have come to value:

**Value 1**

**Individuals and interactions**

**over**

**Processes and tools**

**Value 2**

**Working software**

**over**

**Comprehensive documentation**

**Value 3**

**Customer collaboration**

**over**

**Contract negotiation**

**Value 4**

**Responding to change**

**over**

**Following a plan**

**Important**

Notice that the Manifesto does **not** say:

-   Processes are bad

-   Documentation is useless

-   Contracts are unnecessary

-   Planning should be ignored

Instead, it says:

While the items on the right have value, **we value the items on the
left more.**

This distinction is frequently asked in interviews.

**Value 1: Individuals and Interactions over Processes and Tools**

**Why?**

Processes and tools cannot solve problems by themselves.

People solve problems.

**Real-Life Analogy**

Imagine a hospital.

Would you rather have:

-   The world\'s best hospital software

-   Poor communication between doctors and nurses

OR

-   Good communication between healthcare professionals

-   Average software

Most people would choose the second option.

Good teamwork often produces better outcomes than perfect tools alone.

**Software Example**

Two developers using only email to communicate may misunderstand
requirements.

Two developers discussing the work directly for ten minutes can often
solve the issue immediately.

**Pharmacovigilance Example**

Suppose an AI incorrectly extracts an adverse event.

A pharmacovigilance specialist, developer, and Product Owner discuss the
issue together.

Within minutes they identify the cause and improve the extraction logic.

The conversation creates more value than simply following a predefined
process.

**Interview Tip**

Agile values **people first**, while still recognizing that processes
and tools are important.

**Value 2: Working Software over Comprehensive Documentation**

This value is often misunderstood.

It does **not** mean:

\"Don\'t write documentation.\"

Instead, it means:

Documentation should support the product---not replace it.

**Real-Life Analogy**

Imagine buying a new washing machine.

Which is more valuable?

-   A 500-page manual

-   A washing machine that actually works

The manual has value.

But the working machine provides the real value.

**Software Example**

Traditional approach:

-   300-page requirement document

-   No usable software for a year

Agile approach:

-   Essential documentation

-   Working software delivered every few weeks

**Healthcare Example**

Imagine a hospital receives:

-   A detailed system specification

-   No working patient registration module

The document doesn\'t help patients.

A functioning registration system does.

**AI Pharmacovigilance Example**

Would your users prefer:

-   A 200-page AI design document

or

-   An AI that correctly extracts adverse event information from PDF
    reports?

The second option delivers immediate value.

**Value 3: Customer Collaboration over Contract Negotiation**

Traditional projects often treated the signed contract as the final
authority.

Agile treats the customer as an ongoing partner.

**Real-Life Analogy**

Imagine hiring an architect.

Traditional approach:

Sign the contract.

No further discussions.

Receive the completed house a year later.

Agile approach:

Review progress regularly.

Adjust room sizes.

Improve the layout.

Refine the design together.

The customer remains involved throughout the project.

**Healthcare Example**

Doctors test each release and suggest improvements.

The software evolves around real clinical workflows instead of
assumptions.

**AI Product Example**

Users say:

\"We need confidence scores for AI-extracted adverse events.\"

The Product Owner updates priorities.

The team delivers this improvement in a future sprint.

**Value 4: Responding to Change over Following a Plan**

This is one of Agile\'s defining characteristics.

Plans are useful.

But plans should evolve when reality changes.

**Real-Life Analogy**

You\'re driving from Ahmedabad to Mumbai.

Your GPS reports:

\"Accident ahead.\"

Would you continue on the original route because that was the plan?

Of course not.

You choose a better route.

Agile works the same way.

**Healthcare Example**

A new regulatory guideline requires additional safety reporting fields.

An Agile team updates the backlog and implements the change in upcoming
iterations.

**AI Example**

A significantly better AI model becomes available.

Instead of ignoring it because it wasn\'t in the original plan, the team
evaluates whether adopting it provides more customer value.

**What is an Agile mindset?**

An Agile mindset is a way of thinking that values:

-   Customer value over simply completing tasks.

-   Learning and adaptation over rigidly following a plan.

-   Collaboration over working in silos.

-   Continuous improvement over assuming the first solution is perfect.

-   Experimentation and feedback over making assumptions without
    validation.

Simple definition: Agile mindset means focusing on delivering value,
learning quickly, adapting to change, and continuously improving.

**Why mindset matters more than process**

A team can:

-   Hold Daily Scrums.

-   Use Jira.

-   Write User Stories.

-   Run Sprint Reviews.

But if they still:

-   Ignore customer feedback,

-   Resist change,

-   Work in silos, or

-   Focus only on finishing tasks,

then they are doing Agile without being Agile.

**Real-life analogy**

Imagine two doctors treating patients.

-   Doctor A follows a checklist exactly, even when the patient's
    condition changes.

-   Doctor B follows medical guidelines but also observes the patient,
    adjusts treatment, and learns from the outcome.

Doctor B demonstrates an Agile mindset: use a process, but adapt based
on real evidence.

**Agile mindset vs traditional mindset**

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Traditional mindset**         **Agile mindset**
  ------------------------------- ---------------------------------------
  Requirements are fixed.         Requirements can evolve.

  Follow the original plan.       Adapt based on feedback.

  Success means completing scope. Success means delivering customer

**What is Waterfall?**

The Waterfall model is a linear and sequential approach to project
management and software development.

In Waterfall, work flows through predefined phases:

Each phase is usually completed before the next phase begins. This makes
Waterfall structured and predictable when requirements are stable.

the entire project before delivery, Agile teams build and deliver the
product in small increments.

Build

↓

Test

Get Feedback

↓

Improve

↓

Repeat

**Simple real-life analogy**

**Waterfall analogy: building a bridge**

When building a bridge, engineers must know the exact:

-   Length

-   Height

-   Materials

-   Budget

-   Safety requirements

Changing the bridge design after construction starts can be extremely
expensive and dangerous. Therefore, Waterfall works well for projects
with stable requirements.

**Agile analogy: creating a mobile app**

When building a mobile app, customer preferences may change frequently:

-   Users may want a simpler interface.

-   Competitors may introduce new features.

-   Market trends may shift.

-   Technology may evolve.

In this situation, Agile is more suitable because the team can release
features incrementally and adapt based on user feedback.

**Waterfall advantages**

-   Clear structure: Each phase has defined deliverables and milestones.

-   Predictable scope: Requirements are documented upfront.

-   Strong documentation: Useful for regulatory and compliance-heavy
    projects.

-   Easier budgeting: Costs and timelines can be estimated more clearly
    when requirements are stable.

-   Suitable for fixed-scope projects: Works well when changes are
    unlikely.

**Waterfall limitations**

-   Inflexible to change: Modifying requirements later can be costly.

-   Late feedback: Customers may not see the product until late in the
    project.

-   Higher risk: If requirements are misunderstood, the final product
    may not meet user needs.

-   Long delivery cycles: Customers wait longer to receive usable value.

**Agile advantages**

-   Flexibility: Requirements can evolve as the project progresses.

-   Early value delivery: Customers receive usable features sooner.

-   Continuous feedback: Regular input improves product quality.

-   Reduced risk: Problems are identified and corrected early.

-   Better collaboration: Cross-functional teams work closely together.

**Agile limitations**

-   Less predictability: Scope may change during the project.

-   Requires active customer involvement: Stakeholders must provide
    regular feedback.

-   Can be challenging for fixed contracts: Flexible scope may conflict
    with rigid contractual agreements.

-   Needs strong team discipline: Agile works best when teams are
    collaborative and self-organized.

**When to use Waterfall**

Waterfall is most appropriate when:

-   Requirements are clear, stable, and unlikely to change.

-   The project involves physical construction or manufacturing.

-   Regulatory compliance requires detailed upfront documentation.

-   The project scope, budget, and timeline are fixed.

-   Examples include bridges, buildings, aerospace systems, and some
    highly regulated healthcare systems.

**When to use Agile**

Agile is most appropriate when:

-   Requirements are expected to change.

-   Customer feedback is essential.

-   The project involves software, AI, or digital products.

-   Innovation and rapid learning are priorities.

-   Examples include mobile apps, web platforms, AI systems, and
    evolving healthcare software.

-   "Waterfall has no customer involvement." Customers are involved, but

**Best practices**

-   Choose Agile when requirements are evolving and customer feedback is

-   Use a hybrid approach when some parts of the project require strict

-   Always evaluate the project's risk, complexity, regulatory

produce high-quality products efficiently by eliminating activities that

-   Create flow -- Ensure work moves smoothly through the process.

-   Establish pull -- Produce work only when there is demand.

-   Pursue perfection -- Continuously improve the process.

**What is Kanban?**

Kanban is a workflow management method that helps teams visualize work,
limit work in progress (WIP), and improve the flow of tasks through a
process.

The word Kanban comes from Japanese and means "visual signal" or "card".

**Simple definition**

Kanban means: Visualize work, limit work in progress, and continuously
improve flow.

**Core principles of Kanban**

-   Visualize workflow -- Display work items on a Kanban board.

-   Limit work in progress (WIP) -- Prevent the team from taking on too
    many tasks at once.

-   Manage flow -- Ensure tasks move smoothly from one stage to another.

-   Make process policies explicit -- Clearly define how work moves
    through the system.

-   Implement feedback loops -- Use regular feedback to improve the
    process.

-   Improve collaboratively -- Encourage the team to continuously
    optimize the workflow.

**Key Kanban practices**

**1. Visualize work**

Example: If the "In Progress" column has a WIP limit of 2, the team
cannot start a third task until one of the current tasks is completed.

**3. Manage flow**

Kanban focuses on keeping work moving smoothly through the system. The
team monitors:

-   Cycle time -- Time taken to complete a task once work begins.

-   Lead time -- Time taken from request to completion.

-   Throughput -- Number of tasks completed in a given period.

**Why Agile myths exist**

Important insight: Agile is not just a set of meetings or tools. It is a

continuously improving.

**Myth 1: Agile means no planning**

**Reality**

Agile requires continuous planning, not the absence of planning.

-   Roadmap planning.

**Myth 2: Agile means no documentation**

-   Documentation should provide value.

-   Excessive documentation that delays delivery is discouraged.

**Reality**

-   Sprints are time-boxed.

-   Releases can have target dates.

-   Roadmaps provide long-term direction.

-   Priorities may change, but commitments still matter.

AI product example: An AI Medical Chatbot team may have a target release
date for the first version, while still refining features based on user
feedback.

**Myth 4: Agile means changing requirements every day**

**Reality**

Agile welcomes change, but changes are evaluated, prioritized, and
managed.

-   Not every request is accepted immediately.

-   The Product Owner prioritizes the Product Backlog.

-   The team focuses on delivering the highest-value work first.

Healthcare example: If a new pharmacovigilance regulation requires
additional reporting fields, the Product Owner would prioritize that
change in the backlog rather than disrupting all ongoing work.

**Myth 5: Agile means working faster**

simply working faster.

Why Agile works:

-   Model performance improves through iterative testing.

-   Explain why Agile is suitable for this project.

-   Identify three project characteristics that support the use of
    Agile.

-   Identify two regulatory or compliance considerations that may
    require adaptation of Agile practices.

-   Describe whether a pure Agile approach or a hybrid approach would be
    most appropriate, and explain why.

**Summary**

Agile is most effective for projects where requirements are evolving,
customer feedback is valuable, and incremental delivery is possible.
This makes Agile particularly suitable for software, AI, healthcare
applications, and pharmacovigilance platforms.

However, Agile may be less suitable for projects with fixed
requirements, physical construction constraints, manufacturing
dependencies, or extensive upfront regulatory documentation needs. In
such cases, Waterfall or a hybrid approach may be more appropriate.

**Key takeaways**

-   Use Agile for software, AI, healthcare applications, and projects
    with changing requirements.

-   Use Waterfall or predictive approaches for construction,
    manufacturing, and fixed-scope projects.

-   Evaluate requirement uncertainty, customer feedback, incremental
    delivery, and regulatory constraints before choosing a methodology.

-   Hybrid approaches are often useful in regulated industries such as
    healthcare and pharmacovigilance.

-   Your AI Pharmacovigilance Platform is well suited for Agile because
    it involves evolving requirements, AI improvement, and continuous
    user feedback.
