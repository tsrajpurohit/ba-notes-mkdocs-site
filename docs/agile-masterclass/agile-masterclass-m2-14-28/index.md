**What is an Agile framework?**

An Agile framework is a structured approach that helps teams apply Agile
values and principles in their day-to-day work.

While Agile provides the mindset and philosophy, frameworks provide the
practical structure for implementing that mindset.

Simple definition: An Agile framework is a set of practices, roles,
workflows, and guidelines that help teams deliver value iteratively and
adapt to change.

**Relationship between Agile and frameworks**

Agile

Values and principles

↓

Agile frameworks

Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean, SAFe, etc.

↓

Team practices

Sprints, boards, reviews, retrospectives, flow management

Agile is the philosophy; frameworks are the methods used to apply that
philosophy.

**Why Agile frameworks are needed**

Agile values and principles provide direction, but teams also need
practical guidance on:

-   How to organize work.

-   How to plan and prioritize tasks.

-   How to collaborate with stakeholders.

-   How to track progress.

-   How to improve continuously.

Example:

-   The Agile principle says: "Deliver working software frequently."

-   A framework such as Scrum explains how to do this through Sprints.

-   A framework such as Kanban explains how to do this through
    continuous flow.

**Overview of frameworks**

**Scrum**

-   Uses fixed-length Sprints.

-   Defines roles such as Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers.

-   Includes events such as Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review,
    and Sprint Retrospective.

-   Best for teams that need structure and regular feedback cycles.

**Kanban**

-   Visualizes work on a Kanban board.

-   Uses WIP limits to control work in progress.

-   Focuses on continuous flow rather than fixed iterations.

-   Best for support, maintenance, and continuous delivery work.

**Lean**

-   Focuses on eliminating waste.

-   Improves workflow efficiency.

-   Maximizes customer value.

-   Best for organizations seeking process optimization.

**Extreme Programming (XP)**

-   Emphasizes technical excellence.

-   Uses practices such as pair programming, test-driven development
    (TDD), and continuous integration.

-   Best for projects requiring high code quality and rapid feedback.

**Scaled frameworks**

-   SAFe helps large organizations coordinate many Agile teams.

-   LeSS applies Scrum principles to multiple teams.

-   Nexus focuses on integrating work from several Scrum teams.

-   Spotify model emphasizes autonomous squads and alignment.

**Common misconceptions**

-   "Agile and Scrum are the same." Scrum is one Agile framework, but
    Agile includes many frameworks.

-   "One framework is best for all projects." The best framework depends
    on team size, project complexity, workflow type, and organizational
    context.

-   "Frameworks replace Agile mindset." Frameworks are tools; they are
    effective only when supported by Agile values and principles.

**What is Scrum?**

Scrum is an Agile framework used to develop, deliver, and sustain
complex products through iterative and incremental work.

Instead of building the entire product at once, Scrum teams work in
short cycles called Sprints, usually lasting 1 to 4 weeks.

Simple definition: Scrum is a lightweight Agile framework that helps
teams deliver valuable product increments through collaboration,
transparency, inspection, and adaptation.

**Purpose of Scrum**

Scrum was designed to help teams:

-   Deliver value quickly through small, usable increments.

-   Adapt to changing requirements without disrupting the entire
    project.

-   Improve collaboration between business and technical teams.

-   Increase transparency so everyone understands the work and progress.

-   Continuously improve the product and the team's process.

-   Reduce risk by getting feedback early and often.

**Example**

Imagine building an AI Pharmacovigilance Platform.

-   Traditional approach: Build all modules for 12 months, then release
    the full platform.

-   Scrum approach: Build Login and Dashboard in Sprint 1, get feedback,
    then continue with ICSR Management, AI Data Extraction, and MedDRA
    Coding in later Sprints.

The Scrum approach reduces the risk of building features that users may
not need or use.

**History of Scrum**

**Origin of the term**

The term "Scrum" comes from the sport of rugby, where players work
together closely to move the ball toward a goal.

In rugby, a scrum is a formation where team members collaborate tightly,
adjust to changing conditions, and work as a unit.

**Early inspiration**

In 1986, Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka published an article in
Harvard Business Review titled "The New New Product Development Game".

They observed that successful product development teams:

-   Worked collaboratively.

-   Moved quickly.

-   Adapted to changing conditions.

-   Developed products in overlapping phases rather than strict
    sequential stages.

This article inspired the Scrum framework.

**Formal development of Scrum**

Scrum was formally developed in the early 1990s by:

-   Jeff Sutherland.

-   Ken Schwaber.

They presented Scrum publicly in 1995 and later became co-authors of the
Scrum Guide, which defines the official Scrum framework.

**Scrum timeline**

1986

Takeuchi and Nonaka describe the "Scrum" concept in product development.

1993

Jeff Sutherland begins applying Scrum ideas in software development.

1995

Sutherland and Schwaber present Scrum publicly.

2001

Agile Manifesto is published, and Scrum becomes one of the most popular
Agile frameworks.

2010+

Scrum becomes widely adopted across software, healthcare, finance, and
product development industries.

**Key ideas behind Scrum**

**Empirical process control**

Scrum is based on empirical process control, which means decisions are
made based on observation, experience, and experimentation rather than
assumptions.

Scrum relies on three pillars:

  --------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Pillar**     **Meaning**
  -------------- -----------------------------------------------------------
  Transparency   Everyone can see the work, progress, and challenges.

  Inspection     The team regularly reviews the product and process.

  Adaptation     The team adjusts plans and practices based on feedback and
                 findings.
  --------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Iterative and incremental development**

-   Iterative means the team repeats cycles of planning, building,
    testing, and reviewing.

-   Incremental means each Sprint produces a usable addition to the
    product.

Login and Dashboard

↓

Sprint 2

ICSR Management

↓

Sprint 3

AI Data Extraction

↓

Sprint 4

MedDRA Coding

**Scrum values**

Scrum is guided by five core values:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Value**    **Meaning**
  ------------ -----------------------------------------------------------
  Commitment   The team commits to achieving the Sprint Goal.

  Focus        The team concentrates on the work selected for the Sprint.

  Openness     The team is transparent about progress and challenges.

  Respect      Team members value each other's contributions.

  Courage      The team is willing to face difficult problems and make
               improvements.
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

**When Scrum should be used**

Scrum is most suitable when:

-   The project is complex and evolving.

-   Requirements are likely to change.

-   Customer or stakeholder feedback is important.

-   The product can be delivered in small increments.

-   The team can work collaboratively in short iterations.

-   Continuous improvement is desired.

**Examples of suitable projects**

-   Software development projects.

-   AI and machine learning products.

-   Healthcare software systems.

-   Pharmacovigilance platforms.

-   Mobile and web applications.

-   Product innovation initiatives.

**When Scrum may not be the best choice**

Scrum may be less suitable when:

-   Requirements are fully fixed and unlikely to change.

-   The project involves physical construction or manufacturing.

-   Work is primarily continuous support or maintenance with
    unpredictable incoming tasks.

-   The organization cannot support cross-functional collaboration.

-   Stakeholders are not available for regular feedback.

In such cases, Waterfall, Kanban, or a hybrid approach may be more
appropriate.

**Advantages of Scrum**

-   Faster delivery of value through frequent increments.

-   Better adaptability to changing requirements.

-   Improved collaboration between business and technical teams.

-   Higher transparency through regular events and visible backlog.

-   Continuous improvement through retrospectives.

-   Reduced project risk through early feedback and inspection.

**Limitations of Scrum**

-   Requires active stakeholder involvement.

-   Requires cross-functional teams.

-   May feel too structured for some continuous-flow environments.

-   Can be difficult in organizations with rigid hierarchical
    structures.

-   Requires discipline in following Scrum roles and events.

**Common misconceptions**

-   "Scrum is the same as Agile." Scrum is one Agile framework, not
    Agile itself.

-   "Scrum guarantees success." Scrum improves transparency and
    adaptability, but success depends on team collaboration and proper
    implementation.

-   "Scrum means no planning." Scrum involves continuous planning
    through backlog refinement and Sprint Planning.

**Best practices**

-   Keep the Product Backlog prioritized and transparent.

-   Define a clear Sprint Goal for each Sprint.

-   Ensure the team is cross-functional.

-   Conduct regular Sprint Reviews with stakeholders.

-   Use Sprint Retrospectives to improve the team's process.

-   Deliver a usable product increment at the end of each Sprint.

**Overview of Scrum roles**

The Scrum Team is a small, self-managing, and cross-functional team
responsible for delivering a valuable product increment each Sprint.

According to the Scrum Guide, the Scrum Team consists of:

-   Product Owner -- Maximizes product value.

-   Scrum Master -- Facilitates Scrum and helps the team improve.

-   Developers -- Build the product increment.

**Product Owner**

**Purpose**

The Product Owner (PO) is responsible for maximizing the value of the
product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team.

**Key responsibilities**

-   Define and communicate the Product Goal.

-   Create and manage the Product Backlog.

-   Prioritize backlog items based on business value.

-   Ensure backlog items are clear and understood.

-   Collaborate with stakeholders to gather requirements and feedback.

-   Make decisions about what should be built next.

**Simple analogy**

The Product Owner is like a restaurant owner who decides:

-   What dishes should be on the menu.

-   Which dishes are most important to prepare first.

-   What customers value most.

**Healthcare example**

In a Hospital Management System, the Product Owner may prioritize:

-   Patient registration.

-   Appointment scheduling.

-   Prescription management.

-   Billing integration.

**Pharmacovigilance example**

For your AI Pharmacovigilance Platform, the Product Owner may
prioritize:

-   ICSR Management.

-   AI Data Extraction.

-   MedDRA Coding.

-   Duplicate Detection.

-   Signal Detection.

**Common mistakes**

-   Not prioritizing the backlog clearly.

-   Acting as a project manager instead of a value maximizer.

-   Ignoring stakeholder feedback.

-   Writing unclear backlog items.

**Scrum Master**

**Purpose**

The Scrum Master is responsible for establishing Scrum and helping
everyone understand and apply Scrum theory and practice.

**Key responsibilities**

-   Facilitate Scrum events such as Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint
    Review, and Sprint Retrospective.

-   Remove impediments that block the team's progress.

-   Coach the Scrum Team in Agile and Scrum practices.

-   Help the team become self-managing and cross-functional.

-   Promote continuous improvement.

-   Support collaboration between the Scrum Team and stakeholders.

**Simple analogy**

The Scrum Master is like a sports coach who:

-   Ensures the team follows the rules.

-   Removes obstacles affecting performance.

-   Helps team members improve their skills.

-   Encourages collaboration and discipline.

**Healthcare example**

In a Hospital Management System project, the Scrum Master may:

-   Facilitate discussions between developers and hospital staff.

-   Resolve blockers related to system integration.

-   Ensure Sprint ceremonies are effective.

**Pharmacovigilance example**

For the AI Pharmacovigilance Platform, the Scrum Master may:

-   Remove blockers related to AI model training.

-   Coordinate with regulatory experts.

-   Help the team improve Sprint delivery.

**Common mistakes**

-   Acting as a manager who assigns tasks.

-   Ignoring impediments.

-   Focusing only on meetings instead of team improvement.

-   Not coaching the team in Scrum values and practices.

**Developers**

**Purpose**

Developers are the members of the Scrum Team who are responsible for
creating the product increment each Sprint.

**Key responsibilities**

-   Plan the work needed to achieve the Sprint Goal.

-   Build, test, and integrate product features.

-   Maintain the quality of the product increment.

-   Collaborate with each other and with the Product Owner.

-   Manage their own work and commitments.

-   Inspect progress daily and adapt the Sprint plan as needed.

**Simple analogy**

Developers are like the chefs in a restaurant who prepare the dishes

**What are Scrum events?**

Scrum events are time-boxed activities that create regular opportunities
for the Scrum Team to inspect work, adapt plans, collaborate with
stakeholders, and improve their process.

The main Scrum events are:

-   Sprint -- The container for all other events.

-   Sprint Planning -- Plan the work for the Sprint.

-   Daily Scrum -- Inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal.

-   Sprint Review -- Inspect the Increment and gather stakeholder

**Sprint**

**Purpose**

The Sprint is a fixed-length iteration during which the Scrum Team works
to deliver a usable product Increment.

**Key characteristics**

-   Usually lasts 1 to 4 weeks.

-   Has a clear Sprint Goal.

-   Contains all other Scrum events.

-   Produces a potentially releasable Increment.

-   Maintains a consistent duration throughout the project.

**Sprint Planning**

**Purpose**

-   What work will be done in the Sprint.

-   How the work will be accomplished.

-   What the Sprint Goal will be.

**Key outputs**

-   Sprint Goal.

-   Selected Product Backlog items.

-   Sprint Backlog.

-   Plan for delivering the selected work.

**Participants**

-   Product Owner -- Clarifies backlog items and priorities.

-   Developers -- Estimate capacity and plan the work.

-   Scrum Master -- Facilitates the event.

**Typical questions**

-   Why is this Sprint valuable? (Sprint Goal)

-   What can be completed this Sprint? (Selected backlog items)

-   How will the work get done? (Tasks and plan)

-   Selected items: Appointment form, doctor availability check,

-   Plan: Design UI, build backend API, integrate database, test

**Purpose**

The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute daily meeting for the Developers to
inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan for the next
24 hours.

**Key characteristics**

-   Occurs every day during the Sprint.

-   Time-boxed to 15 minutes.

-   Focused on progress toward the Sprint Goal.

-   Led by the Developers.

-   Helps identify blockers early.

**Common discussion points**

-   What progress has been made toward the Sprint Goal?

-   What work is planned before the next Daily Scrum?

-   Are there any impediments blocking progress?

**Participants**

-   Scrum Team.

**What are Scrum metrics?**

Scrum metrics are measurements that help the Scrum Team understand
progress, forecast future work, and improve their process.

Simple definition: Scrum metrics provide visibility into how much work
has been completed, how much work remains, and how effectively the team

**Why metrics are important**

-   Help the team estimate and plan work.

-   Provide transparency to stakeholders.

-   Identify risks and bottlenecks early.

-   Support continuous improvement.

-   Help forecast future delivery dates.

**Story Points**

**Purpose**

Story Points are a relative measure of effort used to estimate Product

**What Story Points consider**

-   Complexity -- How difficult is the work?

-   Effort -- How much work is required?

-   Risk -- How uncertain or risky is the task?

**Planning Poker**

**Purpose**

Planning Poker is a collaborative estimation technique used by Scrum
Teams to assign Story Points to backlog items.

**How it works**

-   The Product Owner explains a backlog item.

-   Team members discuss the requirements.

-   Each member privately selects a Story Point estimate.

-   All estimates are revealed simultaneously.

-   If estimates differ, the team discusses the reasons.

-   The team repeats the process until a consensus is reached.

**Benefits**

-   Encourages team collaboration.

-   Reduces the influence of dominant personalities.

-   Surfaces hidden assumptions and risks.

-   Improves estimation accuracy.

**AI product example**

For an AI Medical Chatbot:

-   A developer estimates symptom-checking integration as 8 points.

-   A QA tester estimates it as 13 points due to testing complexity.

-   After discussion, the team agrees on 13 points.

**Velocity**

**Purpose**

Velocity measures the amount of work completed by the Scrum Team in a
Sprint, usually measured in Story Points.

**Formula**

Velocity = Total Story Points completed in a Sprint

**Example calculation**

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Sprint**                    **Story Points completed**
  ----------------------------- -----------------------------------------
  Sprint 1                      20

  Sprint 2                      25

  Sprint 3                      22

  Sprint 4                      23

  Average velocity              22.5
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

**How velocity is used**

-   Sprint Planning -- Helps determine how much work the team can
    realistically commit to.

-   Release Planning -- Helps forecast how many Sprints are needed to
    complete remaining work.

-   Team improvement -- Helps the team understand delivery patterns over
    time.

**Important note**

Velocity should not be used to compare teams because Story Point
estimation is relative and specific to each team.

**Burndown Chart**

**Purpose**

A Burndown Chart shows the remaining work in a Sprint over time.

**How to read it**

-   X-axis = Sprint days.

-   Y-axis = Remaining Story Points or tasks.

-   Ideal line = Planned rate of work completion.

-   Actual line = Real progress of the team.

**Example Burndown Chart**

Remaining work (Story Points)

40 ┤●

30 ┤ ●

20 ┤ ●

10 ┤ ●

0 ┤\_\_\_\_●

Day 1 3 5 7 10

Interpretation: The line moving downward indicates that the team is
completing work and reducing the remaining Sprint workload.

**Interpretation**

-   If the actual line is above the ideal line, the team may be behind
    schedule.

-   If the actual line is below the ideal line, the team may be ahead of
    schedule.

-   If the line is flat, little or no work is being completed.

**Pharmacovigilance example**

For the AI Pharmacovigilance Platform, a Burndown Chart can show:

-   Remaining Story Points for AI extraction tasks.

-   Progress of MedDRA coding feature development.

-   Whether the team is on track to meet the Sprint Goal.

**Burnup Chart**

**Purpose**

A Burnup Chart shows the work completed over time and compares it with
the total scope.

**How to read it**

-   X-axis = Time or Sprint days.

-   Y-axis = Story Points.

-   Total scope line = Total work planned.

-   Completed work line = Work completed so far.

Interpretation: The upward line shows cumulative work completed. If the

**Common mistakes**

-   Velocity: Using it as a performance target instead of a planning

-   Burndown Chart: Ignoring the reasons behind deviations from the

-   Planning Poker: Allowing one person to dominate the estimation

-   Metrics: Focusing on numbers instead of delivering customer value.

**Best practices**

-   Use Story Points for relative estimation, not time tracking.

-   Use Planning Poker to encourage collaborative estimation.

-   Use Velocity for Sprint and release forecasting.

-   Review Burndown Charts regularly during the Sprint.

-   Use Burnup Charts when scope changes are common.

**What is Kanban?**

Kanban is a workflow management method used to visualize work, limit
work in progress, and improve the flow of tasks through a process.

The word "Kanban" comes from Japanese and means "visual signal" or
"signboard".

Simple definition: Kanban is an Agile method that helps teams manage and
improve their workflow by making work visible and limiting the amount of
work being done at the same time.

**Purpose of Kanban**

Kanban was created to help teams:

-   Visualize the workflow.

-   Identify bottlenecks.

-   Reduce multitasking.

-   Improve flow efficiency.

-   Deliver work continuously.

-   Adapt to changing priorities.

**Simple analogy**

Imagine a pharmacy dispensing counter:

-   Prescriptions arrive continuously.

-   Each prescription moves through stages: Received → Verification →
    Dispensing → Completed.

-   If too many prescriptions are in Verification, it becomes a
    bottleneck.

-   Limiting the number of prescriptions in verification helps maintain
    smooth flow.

This is similar to how Kanban manages work in software and product
development.

**History of Kanban**

Kanban originated in the Toyota Production System in the 1940s and
1950s.

-   Toyota used visual cards to signal when more materials were needed.

-   This helped reduce waste and improve production flow.

-   Later, Kanban principles were adapted for knowledge work and
    software development by David J. Anderson.

**Core principles of Kanban**

**1. Visualize the workflow**

Make all work visible so the team can understand:

-   What work is waiting.

-   What work is in progress.

-   What work is completed.

**2. Limit Work In Progress (WIP)**

WIP limits restrict the number of tasks allowed in a workflow stage at
one time.

-   Prevents overloading the team.

-   Reduces context switching.

-   Highlights bottlenecks quickly.

**3. Manage flow**

Focus on moving work smoothly from start to completion.

-   Reduce delays.

-   Remove blockers.

-   Improve cycle time.

**4. Make policies explicit**

Clearly define:

-   How work enters the system.

-   What each workflow stage means.

-   When work is considered complete.

**5. Implement feedback loops**

Regularly review workflow performance and identify improvement
opportunities.

**6. Improve collaboratively and evolve experimentally**

Kanban encourages teams to make small, continuous improvements rather
than large disruptive changes.

**What is WIP?**

Work In Progress (WIP) refers to tasks that have been started but not
yet completed.

**Why WIP limits are important**

-   Prevent the team from starting too many tasks at once.

-   Encourage the team to finish existing work before starting new work.

-   Reveal bottlenecks when a column reaches its limit.

-   Improve focus and flow efficiency.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

tasks:

-   Developers cannot move additional tasks into Testing.

-   The team focuses on completing testing work first.

-   This prevents unfinished work from piling up.

**How Kanban improves workflow**

-   Visual transparency -- Everyone can see the status of work.

-   Bottleneck identification -- Blocked stages become visible.

-   Reduced multitasking -- WIP limits encourage focus.

-   Faster delivery -- Work moves continuously through the system.

-   Continuous improvement -- Teams regularly analyze and improve flow.

**1. What is Lean?**

**Simple definition**

**Lean is a methodology focused on maximizing customer value while
minimizing waste.**

In simple words:

Build what customers need, remove unnecessary work, and continuously
improve the process.

**Simple analogy**

Imagine a hospital emergency department.

-   Doctors wait for reports.

-   Staff perform unnecessary paperwork.

Lean thinking asks:

**\"Which steps actually create value for the patient, and which steps
are waste?\"**

**2. Why was Lean created?**

Lean originated from the **Toyota Production System (TPS)**.

After World War II, Japan had:

-   Limited resources.

-   Limited money.

-   High competition.

Toyota needed a way to:

-   Produce high-quality products.

-   Reduce waste.

-   Use resources efficiently.

Toyota developed a system focused on:

-   Continuous improvement.

-   Employee involvement.

-   Eliminating unnecessary activities.

This became known globally as **Lean Manufacturing**.

Later, Lean concepts moved into:

-   Software development.

-   Healthcare.

-   Product management.

-   Startups.

-   AI development.

**3. Lean mindset**

A Lean thinker always asks:

If yes:

Continue.

If no:

Remove or reduce it.

**Question 2:**

\"Can we deliver faster with less waste?\"

**Question 3:**

\"How can we improve continuously?\"

**1. What is Extreme Programming (XP)?**

**Simple definition**

**Extreme Programming (XP) is an Agile software development framework
focused on delivering high-quality software through strong engineering
practices, continuous feedback, and frequent releases.**

In simple words:

Scrum helps teams decide what to build and when to build it. XP focuses
on building it correctly with high technical quality.

**Simple analogy**

Imagine building a hospital.

Scrum answers:

-   Which departments should be built first?

-   What should be delivered in the next phase?

XP answers:

-   Are the electrical systems safe?

-   Are materials high quality?

-   Are construction standards followed?

Both focus on successful delivery, but XP emphasizes **quality of
construction**.

**2. Why was XP created?**

In traditional software development:

Teams often faced problems:

-   Requirements changed frequently.

-   Bugs appeared late.

-   Testing happened at the end.

-   Developers worked separately.

-   Releases were slow.

XP was created to solve these problems by introducing:

-   Continuous testing.

-   Frequent releases.

-   Close collaboration.

-   Better coding practices.

**3. History of Extreme Programming**

XP was created by:

**Kent Beck**

during the late 1990s.

It was first used in a project called:

**Chrysler Comprehensive Compensation System (C3 Project)**

The project faced problems with traditional development approaches.

Kent Beck introduced XP practices to:

-   Improve software quality.

-   Increase developer collaboration.

-   Deliver faster.

The book:

**\"Extreme Programming Explained\"**

was published by Kent Beck in 1999.

**4. Why is it called \"Extreme\" Programming?**

The idea:

Take good software development practices and apply them to the extreme.

Examples:

Normal practice:

\"Test software.\"

XP:

\"Write tests continuously before coding.\"

Normal practice:

\"Review code.\"

XP:

\"Two developers work together continuously.\"

Normal practice:

\"Integrate changes.\"

XP:

\"Integrate code multiple times every day.\"

**4. Courage**

**Meaning**

-   Refactor bad code.

**5. Respect**

**Meaning**

Team members respect each other\'s skills and contributions.

**What is it?**

Two developers work together on the same code.

**Driver**

Writes code.

**Navigator**

Reviews and guides.

**Benefits**

-   Fewer bugs.

-   Better knowledge sharing.

-   Better code quality.

-   Accuracy.

Developers write tests before writing the actual code.

Process:

↓

↓

Improve Code

**Meaning**

Developers frequently merge their code into a shared repository.

Benefits:

-   Problems discovered early.

**4. Continuous Testing**

Testing happens continuously.

-   Unit testing.

-   Automated testing.

**5. Refactoring**

**Meaning**

Improving code structure without changing functionality.

Example:

Slow and difficult to maintain.

Cleaner and faster.

**6. Small Releases**

XP encourages releasing small improvements frequently.

Example:

**7. Collective Code Ownership**

Meaning:

Any developer can improve any part of the code.

Benefits:

-   No dependency on one person.

-   Better collaboration.

**8. Coding Standards**

Teams follow common coding practices.

Benefits:

-   Easier maintenance.

-   Projects took years.

-   Requirements changed.

-   Users received software too late.

-   Business teams were disconnected from developers.

-   More structure.

-   More governance.

-   More predictability.

**4. Core philosophy of DSDM**

Fixed:

-   Scope.

Flexible:

-   Time and cost.

↓

↓

-   Time.

Simple meaning:

Scrum works well for one team. SAFe helps hundreds of teams work
together toward one business goal.

**Simple analogy**

Imagine building a hospital information system.

-   Dependencies caused delays.

-   Leadership lacked visibility.

Example:

Team A builds patient records.

Team B builds billing.

Team C builds analytics.

Without coordination:

-   Integration problems.

-   Duplicate work.

-   Delayed releases.

**1. What is LeSS?**

**Simple definition**

**LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum) is a framework for applying Scrum principles
to multiple teams working on one product while keeping the process as
simple as possible.**

Simple meaning:

LeSS is Scrum expanded to multiple teams without adding unnecessary
management layers.

**Simple analogy**

Imagine building a hospital information system.

**Traditional scaling approach:**

Create separate departments:

-   Appointment team.

-   Billing team.

-   Patient records team.

-   Analytics team.

Each has:

-   Separate priorities.

-   Separate managers.

-   Separate goals.

Problem:

Teams optimize their own area instead of the whole hospital system.

**LeSS approach:**

All teams work toward:

**One product goal:**

\"Build the best hospital information system.\"

One:

-   Product Backlog.

-   Product Owner.

-   Product vision.

**2. Why was LeSS created?**

When organizations scaled Scrum, they often created problems:

-   Too many managers.

-   Too many meetings.

-   Separate priorities.

-   Complex processes.

-   Teams working in isolation.

LeSS creators believed:

Many organizations were scaling Agile by adding more layers instead of
improving collaboration.

LeSS focuses on:

-   Simplicity.

-   Transparency.

-   Whole product thinking.

-   Customer value.

**3. History of LeSS**

LeSS was created by:

**Bas Vodde**

and

**Craig Larman**

They developed LeSS based on:

-   Scrum.

-   Lean thinking.

-   Systems thinking.

The framework became popular for organizations wanting lightweight Agile
scaling.

**4. LeSS Philosophy**

LeSS follows these ideas:

**1. More teams, one product**

Multiple teams should work on the same product.

Not separate products.

Example:

AI Pharmacovigilance Platform:

Wrong approach:

Team A owns AI extraction.

Team B owns reporting.

Team C owns dashboard.

Each creates separate priorities.

LeSS approach:

All teams contribute to one product:

\"AI Drug Safety Platform.\"

**2. Simplify organizational structure**

Reduce:

-   Managers.

-   Handoffs.

-   Approval layers.

**3. Customer focus**

The goal is:

Deliver customer value faster.

**4. Whole-product thinking**

Teams are not feature factories.

They are product builders.

**5. Continuous improvement**

Teams continuously improve.

LeSS has two versions:

**1. Basic LeSS**

**1. What is Spotify Model?**

**Simple definition**

**Spotify Model is an Agile organizational approach that organizes
people into autonomous, cross-functional teams while maintaining
alignment through communities and shared practices.**

Simple meaning:

Small teams decide how to build things, but everyone moves toward the
same company vision.

**Simple analogy**

Imagine a pharmaceutical company developing an AI Drug Safety Platform.

Traditional structure:

Everyone reports through departments:

IT Department

↓

Development Team

↓

Testing Team

↓

Business Team

Problems:

-   Slow decisions.

-   Too many approvals.

-   Teams work separately.

Spotify approach:

Create autonomous teams:

AI Extraction Squad

Signal Detection Squad

Analytics Squad

User Experience Squad

Each squad owns a product area.

**2. Why was Spotify Model created?**

Spotify grew rapidly.

Problems appeared:

-   Too many people.

-   Communication challenges.

-   Slow decision-making.

-   Too many dependencies.

They needed:

-   Speed.

-   Innovation.

-   Team ownership.

-   Scalability.

The goal:

Keep the feeling of a small startup while becoming a large organization.

**3. History of Spotify Model**

The model became famous after engineering leaders at Spotify shared
their organizational approach around 2012.

Important note:

Spotify Model was never officially released as a framework.

Many organizations copied parts of it.

**4. Core Principles of Spotify Model**

**Principle 1: Autonomy**

Teams decide:

-   How to work.

-   Which tools to use.

-   How to solve problems.

Example:

AI team decides:

-   Which ML framework.

-   Which testing approach.

-   Which architecture.

**Principle 2: Alignment**

Autonomy does not mean everyone does whatever they want.

Teams align through:

-   Company goals.

-   Product strategy.

-   Vision.

Example:

Company goal:

\"Reduce drug safety processing time by 60%.\"

Every squad contributes toward this.

**Principle 3: Cross-functional teams**

Teams contain different skills.

Example:

AI Product Squad:

-   Developer.

-   Data scientist.

-   UX designer.

-   QA engineer.

-   Product Owner.

-   Business Analyst.

**Principle 4: Continuous improvement**

Teams regularly improve their processes.
